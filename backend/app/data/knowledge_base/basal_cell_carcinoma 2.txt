Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

Basal cell carcinoma is the most common form of skin cancer, accounting for about 80% of all skin cancers. It develops in the basal cells, which are found in the lower part of the epidermis (outer layer of skin).

Key Characteristics:
- Grows slowly and rarely metastasizes (spreads to other parts of the body)
- Most often appears on sun-exposed areas like face, neck, and arms
- Highly treatable with excellent cure rates when detected early
- Can be locally destructive if left untreated

Common Appearances:
- Pearly or waxy bump
- Flat, flesh-colored or brown scar-like lesion
- Bleeding or scabbing sore that heals and returns
- Pink growth with slightly elevated rolled border and crusted indentation in center

Risk Factors:
- Chronic sun exposure
- Fair skin that burns easily
- History of multiple or severe sunburns
- Age (more common in older adults, but increasing in younger people)
- Male gender (men are twice as likely to develop BCC)
- Previous skin cancer
- Family history
- Exposure to radiation or arsenic
- Weakened immune system

Types of Basal Cell Carcinoma:
1. Nodular BCC: Most common type, appears as a round, pearly bump
2. Superficial BCC: Appears as a flat, scaly patch, often on trunk or limbs
3. Morpheaform/Sclerosing BCC: Appears as a scar-like area, can be harder to detect
4. Pigmented BCC: Contains melanin, may appear brown, blue, or black
5. Infiltrative BCC: Grows deeply into surrounding tissue

Diagnosis:
- Visual examination by dermatologist
- Dermoscopy for enhanced visualization
- Skin biopsy (shave, punch, or excisional) for confirmation
- Rarely requires imaging unless deeply invasive

Treatment Options:
- Excisional surgery: Cutting out the cancer with a margin of normal skin
- Mohs micrographic surgery: Precise technique with highest cure rate, ideal for facial lesions
- Curettage and electrodesiccation: Scraping away cancer cells and using electric current
- Cryosurgery: Freezing cancer cells with liquid nitrogen
- Radiation therapy: For patients who cannot undergo surgery
- Topical medications: For superficial BCCs (5-fluorouracil, imiquimod)
- Photodynamic therapy: Light-activated treatment for superficial lesions
- Targeted therapy: For advanced or metastatic cases (vismodegib, sonidegib)

Prevention:
- Minimize sun exposure, especially during peak hours
- Use broad-spectrum sunscreen daily (SPF 30 or higher)
- Wear protective clothing, including wide-brimmed hats
- Avoid tanning beds and sun lamps
- Regular self-skin examinations
- Annual professional skin checks if high-risk

Prognosis:
BCC has an excellent prognosis with cure rates exceeding 95% with appropriate treatment. Recurrence is possible, particularly with aggressive subtypes or incomplete removal. While BCC rarely metastasizes, it can cause significant local tissue destruction if neglected.

Follow-up:
Patients with one BCC have increased risk of developing additional skin cancers. Regular follow-up examinations are essential, typically every 6-12 months depending on individual risk factors.
