Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

Squamous cell carcinoma is the second most common form of skin cancer, representing about 20% of all skin cancer cases. It develops in the squamous cells that make up the middle and outer layers of the skin.

Key Characteristics:
- More aggressive than basal cell carcinoma but less aggressive than melanoma
- Can metastasize (spread) if not treated, though this is relatively uncommon
- Most often appears on sun-exposed areas
- Early treatment is highly effective

Common Appearances:
- Firm, red nodule
- Flat lesion with a scaly, crusted surface
- Raised growth with a central depression
- Wart-like growth
- Open sore that doesn't heal
- Rough, scaly patch on the lip that may evolve to an open sore

Risk Factors:
- Prolonged sun exposure and UV radiation
- Fair skin, light hair, blue or green eyes
- History of sunburns
- Chronic skin inflammation or injury
- Actinic keratoses (precancerous growths)
- Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection
- Weakened immune system
- Previous radiation therapy
- Exposure to certain chemicals (arsenic, coal tar)
- Smoking (especially for lip SCC)
- Age over 50

Precursor Conditions:
- Actinic keratosis: Rough, scaly patches that can develop into SCC
- Bowen's disease: Considered SCC in situ (confined to epidermis)

Types and Variants:
1. Cutaneous SCC: Most common, arises from sun-exposed skin
2. SCC in situ (Bowen's disease): Confined to the epidermis, not invasive
3. Keratoacanthoma: Rapidly growing variant that may resolve spontaneously
4. Marjolin's ulcer: SCC arising in chronic wounds or burn scars

Diagnosis:
- Clinical examination by dermatologist
- Dermoscopy for detailed visualization
- Skin biopsy (punch or excisional) for histological confirmation
- Lymph node examination if metastasis is suspected
- Imaging (CT or MRI) for high-risk or advanced cases

Treatment Options:
- Excisional surgery: Standard treatment with clear margins
- Mohs micrographic surgery: Preferred for high-risk tumors or cosmetically sensitive areas
- Curettage and electrodesiccation: For small, low-risk tumors
- Cryotherapy: Freezing for very small, superficial lesions
- Radiation therapy: For inoperable tumors or after incomplete excision
- Systemic therapy: For metastatic disease (chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy)
- Lymph node dissection: If lymph nodes are involved

High-Risk Features:
- Tumor diameter greater than 2 cm
- Depth greater than 2 mm or invasion beyond subcutaneous tissue
- Location on ear, lip, or temple
- Poorly differentiated histology
- Perineural invasion
- Immunosuppressed patient
- Arising in scar or chronic wound

Prevention:
- Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 30+)
- Protective clothing and hats
- Avoid tanning beds
- Seek shade during peak sun hours
- Treat actinic keratoses promptly
- Regular self-examinations
- Professional skin checks, especially for high-risk individuals
- Quit smoking

Prognosis:
Most SCCs are curable when detected and treated early. The overall cure rate is about 95% for localized disease. However, metastatic potential exists, particularly for high-risk tumors. Approximately 2-5% of SCCs metastasize, most commonly to regional lymph nodes.

Follow-up:
Patients with SCC require regular monitoring due to risk of recurrence and development of new skin cancers. Follow-up schedules typically involve examinations every 3-6 months for the first few years, then annually.

Metastatic Disease:
When SCC metastasizes, it most commonly spreads to nearby lymph nodes first, then potentially to distant organs. Metastatic SCC requires systemic treatment and has a less favorable prognosis.
